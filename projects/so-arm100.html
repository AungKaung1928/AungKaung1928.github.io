One Arm, Three Ways to Learn It

The SO-ARM100 is the low-cost 5-DOF arm behind most of the open LeRobot datasets. This is the same arm in MuJoCo, taught to reach, push, lift and place a cube three different ways: by reinforcement learning from a reward, by imitation of a scripted demonstrator, and by a small language-conditioned policy that reads the instruction. One shared bench defines the tasks, the physics shifts and the success protocol, so the three tables can be laid side by side.

01

In plain words

What the arm is, what it is asked to do, and why the same task is solved three times.

The SO-ARM100 is a 3D-printed arm with five joints and a parallel gripper, driven by hobby servos. It costs a few hundred dollars and it is the arm most of the open LeRobot community data was recorded on, which makes it the natural target for a project about learning manipulation from data.

Here the arm lives in MuJoCo, a physics simulator, on a laptop with no graphics card. The description comes from MuJoCo Menagerie, unchanged. On top of it sit a table, three coloured cubes, a target marker and three cameras. The policy's job is one of four things: hover the gripper over a cube, push it onto the marker, lift it clear of the table, or carry it to the marker and let go.

Three separate repositories learn those tasks in three different ways, and the point of running all three on one bench is the comparison.

The three routes

Reinforcement learning gets a reward signal and privileged information (the cube's true position) and has to discover the motion itself. Imitation learning gets a few hundred demonstrations from a scripted expert, a camera image and the arm's own joint readings, and copies. The language policy gets the same image and joints plus a sentence, and has to work out from the sentence which cube and which task is meant, including sentences and task-colour pairs it never saw in training.

Where things stand

All four repositories are written, tested and green in continuous integration. The measurement phase, in which every results table on this page gets its numbers from a named script on an idle machine, has not started. Every table below says so explicitly. Nothing here is a claim about the physical arm.

Terms used on this page

SO-ARM100Standard Open Arm 100: a 5-DOF arm plus gripper from TheRobotStudio, driven by position-controlled servos. Version 1.3 of its MuJoCo model is used, from the Menagerie collection.
MJCF / MjSpecMJCF is MuJoCo's XML model format. MjSpec is the programmatic editor for it, used here to attach the table, cubes, marker and cameras to the arm without editing the vendored file.
inverse kinematicsGiven a target pose for the gripper, find joint angles that put it there. Solved iteratively with damped least squares; the scripted expert is built on it.
scripted expertA hand-written controller that reads the cube's true position from the simulator and moves through IK waypoints. It produces demonstrations and reference success rates. It is not a controller a real arm could run.
domain randomisation (DR)Sampling physics parameters (mass, friction, servo gain, latency) from a range at every reset, so a policy is not fitted to one exact model.
held-out physicsFixed physics settings a trained policy is tested on, each outside the DR range. Success there is a transfer claim, not interpolation.
PPOProximal Policy Optimization, the standard on-policy RL algorithm: collect rollouts, estimate advantages, take clipped gradient steps so the policy does not move too far per update.
curriculumTraining on an easier task first and promoting to a harder one once a success threshold is reached, keeping the network weights across the switch.
behaviour cloning (BC)Supervised learning from demonstrations: given what the expert saw, predict what the expert did.
DAggerDataset Aggregation. Let the learner drive, ask the expert what it would have done at the states the learner reached, add those labels, retrain. Fixes the drift BC suffers when it leaves the demonstrated states.
ACT / action chunkingAction Chunking with Transformers: predict a chunk of future actions per forward pass instead of one, and blend overlapping chunks at run time. Used here without the variational latent the original has.
VLAVision-language-action model: a policy conditioned on an image and a sentence. The one here is small and trained from scratch; it is not a fine-tuned foundation model.
sentence embeddingA fixed-size vector for a sentence from a pretrained text model, here MiniLM (384 numbers). Similar meanings should land near each other.
FiLMFeature-wise Linear Modulation: the sentence embedding produces a scale and shift applied to the image features, so the instruction changes what the vision layers look for.
LoRALow-Rank Adaptation: fine-tuning a large network by training small rank-8 matrices added to a few weight matrices, leaving the original weights frozen.
LeRobotDataset v3The on-disk format Hugging Face LeRobot uses for robot demonstrations: parquet tables plus video or image frames, with episode indexing.
02

One bench, three projects

Why the tasks, the physics shifts and the evaluation protocol live in a separate repository that the other three depend on.

A comparison between three learning methods is only worth reading if all three are measured the same way. So everything a policy is later judged against is defined once, in so-arm100-sim: the four tasks and their success rules, the two observation layouts, the DR ranges, the six held-out physics cells, the evaluation protocol (100 episodes × 5 seeds per cell, mean and spread over seeds), the scripted expert and its own success table, the language templates and their train/held-out split, and the dataset recorder.

The three policy repositories install the bench from a pinned release tag (v0.1.0) and import from it. None of them redefines a task or a threshold. A test in the bench hashes the vendored arm description, so an accidental edit to the robot model fails CI rather than silently changing every downstream number.

benchso-arm100-sim Scene, tasks, IK expert, DR, evaluation, instruction templates, dataset writer, vector env. 63 tests · reference numbers measured
route 1so-arm100-rl PPO on privileged state, reach → push → lift curriculum, nominal vs DR, held-out gap, ONNX export. 21 tests · trained, one seed
route 2so-arm100-il BC, DAgger and ACT-lite from scripted demonstrations on image + proprioception; dataset-size curve. 19 tests · not trained yet
route 3so-arm100-vla Language-conditioned multitask policy; seen / paraphrase / combo splits; encoder ablation; LoRA gate. 24 tests · gate measured

What the shared protocol buys: the expert's success rate is the ceiling every learned policy is read against; the RL policy's drop on heavy physics and the imitation policy's drop on the same cell are the same experiment; and "unseen paraphrase" in the language project means exactly the sentences the imitation recorder refused to write.

04

The bench

The tasks, the observations, the physics shifts, and the two things the scripted expert had to get right.

Four tasks, one rule each

Control runs at 20 Hz; each control step is 25 physics steps of 2 ms. Episodes end on success or on the step limit, and the two are reported separately because a value estimate has to bootstrap on one and not the other.

tasksuccessstep limit
reachend effector within 2 cm of a point 3 cm above the cube100
pushcube centre within 2.5 cm (xy) of the marker150
liftcube 5 cm above its resting height and both jaws touching it150
pick_placecube within 2.5 cm of the marker, on the table, released250

The "both jaws touching" clause exists because a cube flung upward otherwise counts as lifted.

Two observation layouts

state, 25-d
Joint angles and velocities, end-effector position, active cube position, cube minus end effector, target position, jaw gap. Privileged: the policy knows where the cube is. Used by the RL project.
proprio, 15-d
Joint angles and velocities, end-effector position. What the real arm knows about itself. Anything about objects has to come from the camera image. Used by the imitation and language projects.

Actions are 6-d either way: delta in [−1, 1] added to the previous joint target, or absolute joint targets in radians, which is what a recorded dataset stores and what the physical arm takes.

Where the cubes may appear, and why the box is small

The fingertip can reach almost anywhere within 0.4 m. Pointing the gripper straight down cannot, because that spends the wrist pitch. tools/reach_map.py maps where a top-down grasp is solvable to 3 mm at the height the lift task has to hold the cube (about 9 cm), and the spawn box, x in ±0.10 m and y in −0.26 to −0.16 m, sits inside that region. The lift height of 5 cm was set from the same map.

Domain randomisation and the held-out cells

Two things are defined here and kept apart. DRConfig is what a training run samples at every reset. EVAL_PHYSICS is a fixed set of shifts a trained policy is tested on, and a test asserts that every cell is outside the DR range in at least one factor.

Scaling kp scales the position actuator's gain and position bias together and leaves the velocity feedback alone, so a weaker motor is also a more damped one. That is what a weaker motor does, and it is a documented choice rather than a side effect.

The scripted expert, and two things a first draft got wrong

The expert reads the cube's true pose from the simulator and moves through IK waypoints, rate limited to 0.05 rad per step so the actions are smooth enough to learn from. It is a demonstrator and a reference, not a controller the real arm could run. Two problems had to be solved before it worked.

1

The grasp is ambiguous by 180 degrees

A parallel jaw closing along yaw θ is the same grasp as θ + π, but only one of the two may be inside the wrist-roll limit from where the arm currently is. Both branches are solved, the better one wins, and the choice is held for the whole episode so the wrist does not flip halfway through the approach.

2

Position servos sag, so the loop had to be closed

The actuators are plain PD with no gravity compensation. The arm settles 1 to 2 cm from the pose IK asked for, more under a weakened kp. The expert integrates the Cartesian error near each waypoint and shifts its IK target by it, with anti-windup, and only once the arm has stopped slewing. A hover phase 1 cm above the cube exists so that this correction has converged before the descent. Without it the open jaw landed on the cube and pushed it away instead of straddling it.

05

Route 1: reinforcement learning

PPO on privileged state, a three-stage curriculum, and the nominal-versus-randomised transfer table.

The RL project asks two measured questions, not one. Does training under randomised physics buy success on physics outside the randomised range? And does a curriculum that starts with reaching buy anything on the lifting task it is meant to help?

The algorithm, and two places it departs from a textbook PPO

The loop is the clipped-surrogate PPO from the author's earlier from-scratch implementation: GAE, orthogonal initialisation, separate actor and critic of two 256-unit tanh layers, learning-rate annealing, 8 forked environments with 128 steps each per update. Two things the cart-pole never forced appear here.

1

Two kinds of episode end

These tasks terminate on success, where there is no future to bootstrap, and truncate on the step limit, where there is: the value of the state the episode stopped in, which the vector env carries as terminal_obs. Treating a truncation as a termination zeroes that bootstrap and corrupts the value target for roughly 1/(1−γ) ≈ 100 steps before every step limit. A four-step test case with one of each is worked by hand.

2

A running observation normaliser, frozen for evaluation

The 25-d state mixes radians, metres and a scaled velocity, and the per-group RMS spans more than an order of magnitude. A Welford running mean and variance is updated from rollouts and then frozen at evaluation and export, so the policy that is measured is the policy that was trained. Actions are clipped to [−1, 1] at the environment boundary while the log-probability uses the unclipped sample; that is biased and common, and the saturation fraction is logged so its size stays visible.

Curriculum

Stages run reach → push → lift. Promotion happens when the rolling success over the last 100 finished episodes reaches 0.8 for reach and push, or 0.6 for lift. Weights and normaliser are kept across the promotion, the learning-rate anneal restarts, and the vector env swaps its task. --no-curriculum trains lift from scratch on the same budget. What should transfer between stages is the joint-space skill of putting the end effector at a point; what should not is anything about contact. Whether the transferred part is worth the steps spent on reach and push is the measurement.

The transfer table

--dr full samples the bench's ranges at every reset; --dr none trains on the vendored model as is. Both policies are then run on every held-out cell, plus a dr cell with in-range sampling, which is the DR policy's home ground. Each cell is 100 episodes × 5 seeds.

Three failed runs, kept

Step size. Plain clipped PPO at 8 epochs per update drove clip fraction to 0.77, approximate KL to 1.44 and σ from 0.50 to 0.054 by 4.46 M steps, with success at 0.02. Fix: stop the epoch loop once KL passes 0.02. Hovering. With the KL stop, lift learned to grasp and then its success fell while return rose: a probe showed the cube held at 4.7–4.9 cm for 138 of 150 steps, just under the 5.0 cm success line, because success ended the episode and the shaping reward for holding was worth more than the bonus. Fix: do not terminate on success during training; every evaluation still counts the first success. DR from scratch. The same recipe under full randomisation stalled: rolling success peaked at 0.09, then sat at exactly 0 for 700 updates up to 4.9 M steps, with normal KL and clip fraction. It was stopped and replaced by fine-tuning the nominal policy under randomisation for 10 M steps. One seed, so that says the recipe failed on seed 0, not that DR from scratch cannot work.

Nominal from scratch crossed 0.6 at 1.0 M steps and finished 20 M in 2 h 10 min, at 3,043 env-steps/s on a quiet host and 2,425 while the Windows side was busy. The exported ONNX policy (the DR fine-tune, 73,990 parameters) matches PyTorch to 2.1e-6 and runs in 0.008 ms p50, 0.015 ms p99 on one thread.

The DR fine-tune halves the mean drop, and almost all of it is one cell: three steps of action latency, one step past the randomised range, costs the nominal policy more than half its success and the DR policy a quarter. Mass and friction shifts cost neither anything. Two things weaken the claim: one training seed per column (the ± is over evaluation seeds only), and the DR column has 10 M more training steps, so part of the gain may be training length. A 30 M-step nominal run is the control that would separate the two, and it has not been run.

A smaller mean drop for the DR policy is evidence that randomising these factors transfers to shifts in these factors. It would say nothing about the physical SO-ARM100, whose unmodelled effects (backlash, cable friction, camera latency) are in neither column. The scripted expert's own numbers on the same cells, from the bench, are the ceiling to read the table against.

Checkpoints and export

Runs are chunked and resumable. The checkpoint carries model, optimiser, normaliser, RNG and curriculum state, and a test asserts that two updates after a reload equal two updates without one, bit for bit. The throughput monitor warns when the rolling env-steps/s drops more than 20% below the first five updates; the machine cannot read its own temperature and CPU contention looks identical, so the warning says to check both. Export folds the normaliser into the actor's mean as one ONNX graph that takes raw observations, verified against PyTorch to 1e-5 on 1,000 random observations before any latency is quoted.

Route 2: imitation

Behaviour cloning, DAgger and a chunked transformer from scripted demonstrations, and the question of how many demonstrations each needs.

Imitation is the route physical arms mostly take: no reward shaping, no privileged state, a few hundred demonstrations and a camera. Here the policy sees what the real arm would see, the 15-d proprioception vector and a 96 × 128 front-camera image, and nothing else.

The expert plays two roles

demonstrator
Successful expert episodes are written as a LeRobotDataset v3: proprio, front image, the expert's absolute joint targets as the action, and an instruction drawn from the training templates only.
oracle
DAgger needs a label at states the learner visits, which a human demonstrator cannot give after the fact. A scripted expert can: asked at any state, it returns what it would do from there, its phase machine advancing on the true state. A test checks that with the mixing coefficient at 1 the labels equal what the expert alone would have done.

Three models, one loss

modelinputoutputwhy it is there
StateMLP15-d proprio1 actionThe floor. Proprioception alone cannot locate the cube; this is the number the image models must beat.
ImagePolicyimage + proprio1 actionA small CNN ending in a spatial soft-argmax over 16 keypoint channels. The last layer is a coordinate, so position is handed to the network rather than learned. Keypoints and proprio feed an MLP.
ACTLiteimage + propriochunk of 20 actionsACT without the variational latent: keypoint tokens and a proprio token through a 2-layer encoder, 20 learned queries through a 2-layer decoder. Overlapping chunks are blended at run time.

All three train with L1 on normalised actions. Mean-squared error fits the conditional mean, and when demonstrations are multimodal (two valid grasp yaws, two ways round a cube) the mean is a third, invalid action. L1 fits the conditional median, which is one of the modes, and it is what ACT trains with. Padded chunk entries are masked. Normalisation statistics come from the training episodes only and travel with the checkpoint. The split is by episode, never by frame, and the final epoch is reported rather than the best one on validation.

Route 3: language

A small policy that reads a sentence, and three held-out splits that test whether it read it or memorised it.

The interesting property of a vision-language-action model is that one policy follows language across tasks and objects and generalises to phrasings and combinations it was not trained on. That property can be tested at about one million parameters on a laptop CPU with a real held-out protocol. What cannot be tested here is scale, and the repository does not pretend otherwise.

What the policy is

Three tokens go into a 128-wide, 2-layer transformer encoder: a frozen 384-d sentence embedding, 32 image keypoints from a small CNN, and the arm's proprioception. Ten learned queries go through a 2-layer decoder and come out as a chunk of ten joint-target vectors, blended at run time as chunks overlap. Before the keypoints are pooled, the sentence embedding produces a per-channel scale and shift on the image features (FiLM), so "the blue cube" can sharpen blue before position is read out. Training is L1 on normalised actions over a few thousand expert frames covering four tasks and three colours, all three cubes always on the table.

What it is not

It is not a foundation model and it does not fine-tune one. SmolVLA-base appears as a zero-shot calibration line, inference only. A LoRA fine-tune of a SmolVLM-sized network is gated on a measured compute check and is future work even where the gate passes.

The three splits

Sentences and (task, colour) pairs come from the bench's instruction module, so "unseen" means the same sentences the imitation recorder never writes. There are five training and three held-out templates per task, for example training "pick up the {c} cube" and held-out "hoist the {c} block". Nine of the twelve (task, colour) pairs are used in training; lift:blue, push:red and pick_place:green are held out.

Does the language model matter? An ablation with a control

Two encoders produce the same 384-d shape. MiniLM (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 22M parameters, frozen) is a sentence model that should place "hoist the blue block" near "lift the blue cube". A hashed bag of words and bigrams has no parameters and no semantics; "hoist" and "lift" land in unrelated buckets. If the hashed policy scores as well as MiniLM on paraphrase, the split was not testing language understanding. If MiniLM wins there and ties on seen, the difference is the encoder's contribution and nothing else, because policy, data and seeds are identical. A third row removes FiLM, leaving language as a token only.

Measured, and weak

The policy barely works: reach about a quarter of the time, push, lift and pick-place between 0 and 7%. Every encoder row sits inside the others' spread, so the ablation cannot separate them; with the base policy this close to the floor there is no headroom for the encoder to show. The training record says why: final L1 on held-out demonstration episodes is 0.128 against 0.057 on training ones, from about 20 demonstrations per (task, colour) pair. The policy does not generalise from its demonstrations before language enters, so the next experiment is more data per pair, not a different encoder. Protocol deviations: 20 episodes per seed instead of 100 and one training seed per encoder instead of three, both for evaluation time (1.7 hours per policy on this CPU).

One thing already measured about the encoder

Cosine similarities of MiniLM embeddings on these templates, recorded by a test rather than asserted away: "lift the green cube" is 0.59 from its paraphrase "raise the green block into the air" and 0.38 from "hoist the green block", but 0.63 from "push the red cube to the target" and 0.82 from "lift the blue cube". The encoder is driven by shared surface words, and the colour word moves the embedding very little. So the paraphrase split is hard for a reason that sits in the encoder, and the combo split asks the policy to read colour out of a small difference between two nearly parallel vectors. Whether FiLM on the image features is enough to do that is what the ablation measures.

SmolVLA-base, zero-shot, same simulator

lerobot/smolvla_base was pretrained on community SO-100/101 data: real arms, real cameras, motor units. Here it receives a rendered 96 × 128 frame letterboxed to its 512 × 512 input, six joint angles in radians zero-padded to its state width, and the instruction. Nothing is adapted and the expected result is low. It is a calibration line, not a comparison of capability: different embodiment statistics, camera placement, units and control rate make it evidence about this protocol, not about SmolVLA. A dry-run mode validates the observation mapping against a stub with the same feature keys, so the test suite covers it without the download.

Measured over 10 episodes per task: reach 0.00, push 0.00, lift 0.00, pick_place 0.00, 0 of 40 as expected. One 50-step action chunk takes 3.16 s at the median (p90 3.30 s, n = 130) on 8 CPU threads, 64 times the 50 ms control period: on CPU a 500M-parameter VLA is not a real-time controller, whatever it scores after fine-tuning.

The LoRA gate

Before proposing a fine-tune, measure whether one step of it is affordable. The gate builds a decoder with SmolVLM-256M's language-model shape (hidden 576, 30 layers, 9 heads, MLP 1536), attaches rank-8 LoRA to the query and value projections, and times one forward-backward-optimiser step at batch 1, sequence 256, on 8 threads. The rule was fixed before measuring: go under 5 s per step, otherwise no-go. At 5 s per step, 2,000 steps is 2.8 hours, the most a chunked, attended CPU run can carry.

Measured: a stand-in of 176.7M parameters with 0.55M trainable (0.31%), 0.51 s per step on 8 threads, best of two, so the gate passes and 2,000 steps would be about 0.3 h. The gate times the language-model half only; the real SmolVLA action expert adds a vision tower and a flow-matching head that were not timed. A pass says a fine-tune is affordable to try, not that it would help.

Limits, stated

What these four repositories cannot show, regardless of what the tables end up saying.

  • Simulation only. No number is a claim about the physical SO-ARM100. Backlash, cable friction and camera latency are in none of the columns.
  • Fixed cameras. Front, side and top; no wrist camera yet. That is an open issue in three of the four repositories.
  • Three cubes of three colours, one active at a time. Nothing about clutter, occlusion or multi-object reasoning.
  • The expert is a script. A policy that imitates an IK script inherits the script's style and its failure modes; it re-aims a push but cannot recover a cube that has yawed off the line.
  • Language is templated. Negation, spatial relations and counting are outside the set.
  • The RL policy is state-based. It sees the cube's true position; image-based control is the other two projects' problem.
  • The DR ranges and held-out cells are one choice. A different choice gives a different table.
  • CPU budget. Eight threads, chunked attended runs, software rendering at about 20 ms per frame. Model sizes and dataset sizes were chosen to fit that, and the throughput monitor cannot tell power limiting from another process on the machine.

The short version

The four repositories in one paragraph.

Four repositories teach a simulated SO-ARM100 to reach, push, lift and place a cube. The first is a bench: the Menagerie arm untouched, a tabletop attached through MjSpec, four tasks with one success rule each, a scripted IK expert that had to resolve a 180-degree grasp ambiguity and close the loop on servos that sag 1 to 2 cm, domain-randomisation ranges, six held-out physics cells each proven outside those ranges, and one evaluation protocol of 100 episodes × 5 seeds. The other three learn on it in three ways. PPO on privileged state with a reach → push → lift curriculum, trained with and without randomisation and tested on the held-out cells, with truncation and termination kept apart in the value target. Behaviour cloning, DAgger with the scripted expert as oracle, and a chunked transformer, all from a camera image and proprioception, on a dataset-size curve from 10 to 200 demonstrations. And a one-million-parameter language-conditioned policy with a frozen MiniLM encoder modulating the image features, tested on held-out paraphrases and held-out task-colour pairs against a hashed-words control, with SmolVLA-base as a zero-shot calibration line and a LoRA fine-tune gated on a step timing that came in at 0.51 s. All of it is written, 122 tests are green, and every results table is empty on purpose until the measurement phase fills it from a script.

Built from the source in so-arm100-sim, so-arm100-rl, so-arm100-il and so-arm100-vla: their README.md files, docs/ISSUES.md, verify.sh, and the packages so_arm100_sim, so_arm100_rl, so_arm100_il and so_arm100_vla.
